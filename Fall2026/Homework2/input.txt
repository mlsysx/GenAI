the campus notebook

maya reached the library before the morning rain. she shook the water from her jacket and placed her notebook on a dry table by the window. the first page held a question rather than a plan: what could the group finish before friday? outside, a student crossed the courtyard with a cardboard model under one arm. inside, the room was quiet except for a cart moving between the shelves. maya divided the page into three small boxes. one box was for ideas, another for evidence, and the last for things nobody understood yet. she left most of the last box empty, knowing that it would fill quickly.

at the next table, eli was preparing for a quiz. he had copied every definition from his notes, but the page still felt unfamiliar. instead of reading it once more, he closed the notebook and tried to explain one idea aloud. halfway through the explanation, he stopped. the missing step was suddenly obvious. he wrote a new question in the margin and worked through an example with a pencil. when a friend arrived, eli asked for a different example rather than an answer. the two examples did not look alike, but the same principle connected them. that connection was easier to remember than the original paragraph.

the student workshop opened with a squeak from the heavy door. jo placed a wooden frame on the central bench and checked the corners with a small square. the frame looked straight from above, yet one corner lifted when she pressed the opposite edge. she resisted the urge to tighten every screw. first she drew the shape of the gap and marked which pieces touched the table. a thin strip of cardboard showed where the problem began. by lunchtime, the frame rested evenly. jo kept the strip beside the finished work because it explained the repair more clearly than a photograph of the final result.

a sign near the courtyard invited students to exchange books. someone had arranged novels, field guides, and a dictionary on a folding table. the dictionary was heavier than the rest, and its cover had almost separated from the binding. a student searching for a travel guide opened it by accident and found a pressed leaf between two pages. no name explained who had left it there. the students decided not to remove the leaf. they placed a small note beside the book describing what they had found, then moved the whole table away from the approaching rain. by afternoon, most of the books had new readers.

sam arrived at the media room with a recording that sounded fine through headphones. when the group played it through the room speakers, a low hum covered the quietest voices. they listened to the opening again and wrote down when the hum began. then they tried a second cable, a different socket, and a shorter recording. only one change removed the problem. nobody needed to guess which microphone had failed because the microphones had not failed at all. sam added the successful setup to the project notes. the group spent the remaining hour adjusting the volume of the voices rather than repeating the whole interview.

the garden club measured a narrow strip of soil behind the studio. the space looked large enough for several rows, but a tree shaded half of it after lunch. lina marked the sunny area on a sketch instead of trusting her first impression. another student brought photographs taken in the morning and evening. together, the pictures told a different story from the single view in front of them. the club chose a smaller planting area and left a path along the wall. they had not planted anything yet, but the path already made the plan feel practical. someone could now reach the far corner without stepping on the beds.

when the project group met on tuesday, everyone brought a different version of the same slide. the numbers matched, but the titles did not. one title claimed that the method was faster; another claimed that it was better. maya asked what the group had actually measured. the answer was time on a short task, not overall quality. they changed the title and added a sentence describing the test. the slide became less dramatic, but easier to defend. at the end of the meeting, they agreed that each claim should point to a result, and each result should say how it was obtained.

a bicycle stood upside down outside the student center. its owner turned the rear wheel and listened to a soft click at the same point on every rotation. a friend offered to adjust the chain, but the owner first marked the wheel with chalk. the click returned when the mark passed the brake. a small piece of paper caught between the rim and the pad revealed the contact. the repair took less time than the discussion about it. before leaving, they spun the wheel again and checked both brakes. the chalk mark stayed on the rim as a temporary reminder of what they had tested.

nora volunteered to organize the shared folder for a class project. the folder contained files called final, final new, and final really new. several had the same date but different contents. she did not delete anything immediately. instead, she created an archive and asked each teammate which file they had last edited. the answers produced a simple timeline. the group chose one current version and put a short description beside it. nobody enjoyed the cleanup, but the next meeting began without a search for missing figures. nora suggested a naming rule that included the topic and a short version number, not a prediction that the work was finished.

the photography class walked around the same building for an hour. from one side, the windows reflected a bright sky. from the other, the wall looked flat and almost colorless. the instructor asked the students to photograph the entrance without changing their camera settings. their results differed because their positions differed. a student who had blamed the camera stepped into the shade and tried again. the exercise did not produce a single perfect photograph. it produced a set of comparisons. later, the students placed the images in order and explained what changed between them: direction, distance, light, and the amount of the building included in the frame.

at a quiet corner of the cafeteria, two students planned a poster session. they listed the questions visitors might ask before choosing the largest picture. one visitor might know the field well; another might only have a minute between classes. the students built two paths through the poster. a short path explained the problem and main result, while a longer path led through methods and limitations. they tested the layout on a friend from another department. she found the title quickly but missed the conclusion. moving one paragraph solved that problem more effectively than making every line of text larger.

the robotics team had a cart that stopped a little short of the marked line. each attempt ended in nearly the same place. the team first checked whether the line itself had moved. then they measured the stopping distance with the same ruler and recorded the floor surface. a loose wheel changed the result more than the software setting they had planned to adjust. after fixing it, the cart reached the line more consistently. the team kept the earlier measurements, clearly labeled as results from the old setup. the difference between the two runs became part of their explanation rather than an embarrassing detail to hide.

a rainstorm forced the outdoor rehearsal into a classroom. the performers carried chairs against the wall and marked the stage with removable tape. the room was narrower than the courtyard, so two entrances had to become one. instead of repeating the scene exactly, they discussed which movements mattered to the audience. a long walk across the stage could become a pause beside a chair. the meaning stayed, even though the arrangement changed. when the rain stopped, nobody hurried outside. the smaller space had revealed a problem with the timing, and the group wanted to fix it before returning to the original plan.

omar brought a homemade lunch to the study room and discovered that he had forgotten a spoon. his friends proposed several elaborate solutions before someone remembered the kitchen downstairs. the small interruption became a joke about their engineering project. they had spent the morning designing a complicated attachment for a problem that might need only a wider handle. after lunch, they tested the simple version first. it did not solve everything, but it showed which part actually needed improvement. omar wrote a note above the sketch: try the smallest useful change before building the largest possible system. the group returned to that note several times.

in the language exchange group, a familiar word caused an unexpected misunderstanding. one student used it to describe a room, while another understood it as a comment about a person. they wrote two example sentences and compared the surrounding words. the dictionary definition alone had not explained the difference. by the end of the conversation, everyone could invent a new example that made the intended meaning clear. the group kept a list of such moments, not as a record of mistakes but as a collection of useful questions. the next week, they began with another word that seemed simple until someone tried to use it.

the makerspace had a shelf of unfinished objects: a wooden bridge, a plastic hinge, a fabric pouch, and a wheel with no axle. each object had a card explaining its current state. one card said only broken, which gave the next person little help. jo replaced it with a description of the visible crack and the conditions under which it appeared. another card listed a successful test but no dimensions. the owner added them after a quick measurement. the shelf did not become tidier, but it became easier to use. students could now continue a project without asking the original builder to remember every step.

a group preparing a short survey argued about the first question. one version asked whether students liked the new design. another asked which of two designs made a task easier. they realized that the questions measured different things. a design could be attractive and still be difficult to use. the group kept both questions but separated them and added a space for comments. during a small practice session, a participant misunderstood one button label. that single observation changed the next draft. the group recorded the change before collecting the main responses so that later results would not silently mix two different versions of the interface.

the evening shuttle was delayed, and a line formed under the shelter. a student checked a saved timetable while another read a message posted beside the stop. the two sources disagreed. rather than announce a departure time as certain, they told the group what each source said and when it had been updated. someone called the information desk, which confirmed the temporary change. the wait was still inconvenient, but the conversation became calmer once the uncertainty was explicit. maya used the time to revise her notes. she crossed out a sentence that sounded confident but had no supporting observation, then replaced it with a question to check later.

an old map hung near the entrance to the exhibition room. its labels were clear, but one corridor no longer led where the map suggested. the student guides noticed visitors repeatedly turning back from the same door. they placed a temporary note beside the map and sketched the current route on a small card. a new visitor tested the card without help. when the visitor reached the right room, the guides knew the instructions worked for at least one person unfamiliar with the building. they still watched for other mistakes. a successful trial was useful evidence, not a reason to stop paying attention.

ravi used a spreadsheet to track materials for a design project. one column mixed centimeters and millimeters, though every value looked like a plain number. the model built from those measurements was far too large. ravi checked the input rows before blaming the drawing program. once the units were separated, the mistake was easy to find. he added the unit to each column heading and wrote a small check for values outside the expected range. the next version of the model fit on the table. the group kept a photograph of the oversized version because it made the importance of units impossible to forget.

at the library help desk, a student asked for everything about renewable energy. the librarian asked what the student needed to produce: a short presentation, a technical report, or background for a discussion. the student chose a presentation for classmates. that answer changed the search. instead of collecting dozens of sources, they selected a few that explained one clear question from different angles. the student left with less material and a better plan. later, she used the same approach with her project group. asking what the next decision required was more helpful than asking for every possible fact about the whole subject.

the art club prepared a display from paper cutouts. under the bright workshop lights, the colors looked distinct. in the dim hallway, two shades became almost impossible to tell apart. the club tested the display in its actual location before mounting it permanently. they added patterns to the pieces so that color was not the only way to distinguish them. one member also suggested labels that could be read from a distance. the final design was not the original design with brighter paint. it was a design that used several kinds of information together, making the same message easier to understand under different conditions.

during a practice presentation, eli spoke faster each time he reached the difficult slide. his teammates timed the talk and noticed the pattern. they asked him to explain that slide without looking at it. the explanation was clearer than the slide itself. together, they replaced a crowded diagram with three stages and added one concrete example. eli still needed to practice, but he no longer rushed through the central idea. the group learned something about rehearsal: it was not only a way to memorize words. it was also a test of whether the material had been organized in a way that a listener could follow.

a student found a charger on a bench and brought it to the campus lost-and-found table. the volunteer could see the connector and color, but not the owner's name. she wrote down those visible features and where it had been found. another student suggested guessing the laptop brand from the shape. the volunteer left that field blank instead. when the owner arrived, the place and time of the loss helped confirm the match. the table's notebook had a column for unknown details, which made the blank field look intentional rather than careless. recording less information had produced a more reliable record.

the group project included a small experiment with paper airplanes. students wanted to compare two folding patterns, but their first trials used different paper and different launch positions. the results were hard to interpret. on the second afternoon, they used the same paper, marked a launch line, and took turns with both designs. the distances still varied. that variation became part of the result rather than something to erase. they reported the typical distance along with the spread. the better pattern was not the one that won a single lucky throw. it was the one that performed well across the set of comparable attempts.

sara kept a journal of her walks between classes. one route was shorter on the map but slower when the main entrance was crowded. another passed a shaded courtyard where she could sit for a moment. she began recording why she chose a route rather than only how long it took. the entries showed that shortest and best were different questions. on a day with little time, distance mattered most. on a hot afternoon, shade mattered more. the journal was not a perfect study, but it helped sara explain her own decisions. a single number could describe part of the experience without describing all of it.

the music students recorded the same melody in two rooms. the first had bare walls and a high ceiling. the second contained curtains, chairs, and a thick rug. they used the same instrument and microphone, yet the recordings sounded noticeably different. a listener described one as brighter and the other as closer. the students wrote down the room conditions before changing the recording settings. they wanted to know which difference came from the space and which came from the equipment. later, they combined a short recording with a diagram of the room, so another group could understand the comparison without being there.

a notebook left open in the lab showed a page full of arrows and crossed-out numbers. its owner apologized for the mess. maya asked which part had changed after the latest measurement. the owner pointed to three entries, and the page suddenly became understandable. they added a date and a short explanation beside each change. the notebook was still untidy, but the history was visible. rewriting it as a flawless final page would have hidden the reasoning that mattered. when the group prepared the report, they used a clean figure for the result and kept the notebook as evidence of how they reached it.

the campus newspaper needed a caption for a photograph of a crowded table. the editor could identify several books and a sign, but the sign's smaller text was unreadable. an early caption described the students as studying for an exam, although nobody had confirmed that. the editor changed it to describe only what the photograph showed. a reporter then asked the students about the event and added the verified context separately. the final caption distinguished observation from explanation. it was less imaginative than the first version, but a reader could tell which details came from the image and which came from a conversation.

the design studio held a challenge to build a container from one sheet of paper. some students began with elaborate folds, while others tested simple shapes. the best-looking container tipped over when filled with small stones. a plain folded tray stayed stable but was difficult to carry. the students compared the designs against three goals: stability, capacity, and ease of handling. no design won every category. for the final demonstration, each team stated which goal it had prioritized and what it had sacrificed. the exercise turned a competition for a single winner into a discussion of trade-offs that could be tested rather than merely asserted.

a student assistant cataloged the tools in a cabinet. several looked almost identical until they were placed beside a ruler. the labels listed their sizes, but two labels had been switched. the assistant checked the markings on the tools before updating the list. she photographed each drawer after arranging it, then compared the photograph with the inventory. one missing item turned out to be behind the divider. the process was slow the first time. the next week, another assistant used the same layout and found a misplaced tool in minutes. consistency had made the routine easier without making it automatic.

in the computing lab, an error message appeared only after the program processed the last record. the team had tested the first few examples many times, so they had assumed the input format was correct. they printed the failing record and found an empty field where a number was expected. the fix was small, but the lesson was larger than the fix. the team added tests for empty inputs, short inputs, and unexpected values. their program did not become cleverer by ignoring those cases. it became more useful by handling them explicitly and explaining what information was missing when it could not continue.

an afternoon workshop asked students to explain a familiar task to someone who had never done it. one student described making tea, another described borrowing a library book, and a third described saving a file. the instructions sounded complete until a partner tried to follow them literally. where should the file be saved? which button confirmed the action? how would the user know it had worked? the students added those details without turning the instructions into a long essay. the best revision usually came from watching the partner hesitate. a moment of confusion pointed directly to a missing piece of the interaction.

the astronomy club planned an observation night, but clouds covered the sky just after sunset. the organizers had prepared a second activity using printed sky maps and a lamp. they explained the difference between the map's idealized view and what a visitor might actually see from the courtyard. people still asked questions, and several stayed longer than expected. the backup activity did not pretend that the weather had cooperated. it addressed a different goal: helping visitors learn how to look when a clear night arrived. after the event, the club separated attendance, learning, and viewing conditions in its notes instead of treating them as one outcome.

a student borrowed a camera and returned with several beautiful but blurred photographs. the blur appeared strongest in the darkest rooms. she compared the exposure settings with those from a brighter hallway and saw that the camera had used a slower shutter. for the next attempt, she steadied the camera and changed one setting at a time. some images improved, while others became too dark. she kept both results in the contact sheet. the trade-off made more sense when she could see it. a setting that helped one property could hurt another, so the final choice depended on what the photograph needed to communicate.

the student council tested a new sign-up form with five volunteers. the form accepted every response, but that did not mean the responses were useful. one volunteer entered a nickname, another placed a phone number in the wrong field, and two skipped a question they did not understand. the team rewrote the labels and added short examples beside the fields. they also explained why each piece of information was requested. the second trial produced fewer questions. the team had not changed the underlying storage system. clearer instructions and more predictable feedback had improved the quality of the information going into it.

a small fountain near the courtyard made a steady sound during the day. students working nearby barely noticed it until the fountain stopped. the sudden quiet made every conversation seem louder. jo used the moment to explain why a background condition should be recorded during an experiment. the condition might feel invisible when it stayed constant, yet its absence could change the experience. her group added a brief description of their testing environment to the report. they did not list every object in the room. they focused on conditions that could plausibly affect the result, including sound, lighting, seating distance, and interruptions.

the team making a campus guide disagreed about how much detail to include on the first page. one person wanted every building name. another wanted only the main route. they printed two versions and asked new students to find the same destination. the detailed page was useful once readers knew where to look, but the simpler page helped them start. the team combined a clear overview with a separate detailed map. the result was not a compromise that made both pages crowded. it was a sequence in which each page answered a different question at the moment a reader was likely to ask it.

at a repair workshop, a student described a device as not working. the volunteer asked what happened when it was switched on. the student explained that the light appeared, the motor started, and then the motor stopped. those observations narrowed the next check without requiring a guess about the cause. the volunteer repeated the sequence once and wrote it down. another device on the table had a similar appearance but a different symptom. keeping the descriptions separate prevented an easy mistake. the students learned to begin with what they could observe, then distinguish a possible explanation from something they had actually confirmed.

the class built a small bridge from sticks and string. before the final test, one team added extra string wherever the bridge looked weak. another team first measured how the bridge bent under a light load. the measurements showed that the most visible bend was not the point where failure began. the second team reinforced a less obvious joint and tested again. both teams could describe what they had changed, but only one had a clear comparison showing why the change mattered. during the discussion, the instructor asked for the earlier photograph as well as the final one. the sequence told the useful part of the story.

a student writing an essay saved a sentence from an article but forgot to mark it as a quotation in her notes. several days later, she could not remember whether the wording was hers. she returned to the article before using the sentence. then she reorganized her notes into three parts: direct quotations, summaries, and her own questions. the distinction took little effort to maintain once it was visible. her next draft used fewer borrowed phrases and more explanation. she could still point to the source of each factual claim. the notes supported her reasoning without making the boundaries between sources and authorship disappear.

the environmental club collected temperature readings from several shaded and sunny locations. one sensor showed an unusually high value near a wall. the students checked the placement and found that the sensor itself was receiving direct sunlight. they recorded the problem rather than quietly replacing the value. a second reading under the planned conditions was lower. the report explained why the first value was excluded from the main comparison and preserved it in a separate log. this made the correction visible. the goal was not to produce a perfectly smooth table, but to show which observations answered the question they had set out to study.

in the computer room, a student finished a long calculation and immediately started another program. the next run took much longer, even though the input had not changed. a teammate checked the machine and found that both jobs were using the same resources. the group repeated the timing under comparable conditions and recorded what else was running. they did not claim that the faster time would occur on every computer. instead, they described the setup clearly enough for another student to understand the measurement. the result became less mysterious once elapsed time was treated as something to measure carefully, not simply a number printed at the end.

a student group designed a game with colored cards and simple rules. during the first play session, one player interpreted a symbol differently from the others. the group had assumed that the symbol was obvious because they had drawn it themselves. they replaced it with a short label and watched a new player try the game. the new player understood the action but missed when a turn ended. a second revision added a clear signal for that transition. the group learned that usability was not a property they could declare in advance. it had to be checked through the behavior of people encountering the design for the first time.

the class discussion moved quickly until someone asked what the word efficient meant in the current argument. one student meant less time, another meant less energy, and a third meant less work for the user. all three could be reasonable goals, but they were not interchangeable. the group rewrote the question with a specific measure and a stated constraint. disagreement did not vanish, yet it became easier to locate. they could now compare proposals without changing the meaning of success halfway through the conversation. maya wrote the final wording at the top of the shared notes so that later decisions would refer to the same question.

a team preparing a demonstration chose a short example because it worked reliably on their laptop. during rehearsal, they also tried a longer input and an empty one. the longer input exceeded the space reserved for the display; the empty one produced an unhelpful message. neither problem changed the main idea of the project, but both affected whether someone else could use it. the team added a clear length limit and a useful response for missing input. at the demonstration, they explained those limits before starting. visitors could then judge what the system did well without mistaking a carefully chosen example for a complete test.

the campus radio group interviewed a student about a new club. a short answer seemed negative when played alone, but the surrounding conversation showed that the student was describing an early difficulty that had already been resolved. the editor kept enough context for the meaning to remain clear. cutting the recording more aggressively would have saved a few seconds while changing the message. the group checked the final version against the original interview and asked whether a listener could distinguish the problem from its resolution. editing, they decided, was not only removing material. it was preserving the relationships that made the remaining material accurate.

a student found two summaries of the same event in a shared document. one focused on the number of attendees, while the other described the questions they asked. the organizers had originally wanted to know whether the event helped new students find research opportunities. attendance alone could not answer that question. the group added a short follow-up form asking what visitors did after the event. they also kept the attendance count, since it answered a different useful question. the final report did not force every observation into one score. it explained which part of the event each measure described and which conclusions remained uncertain.

the lab cabinet had three identical-looking containers, each with a handwritten label. one label had faded until only the first letter remained. the student assistant did not fill in the missing word from memory. she set the container aside and asked the person responsible for the cabinet to check the record. the delay was inconvenient, but guessing would have created a false label that later users might trust. after the check, the team replaced all three labels with clearer versions. they also noted the date. the improved system made uncertainty visible before it became a confident but unsupported description.

a group learning to edit video compared two versions of a short scene. the faster cut felt exciting, but several viewers missed the action that explained the ending. the slower cut made that action clear but lost momentum in the middle. the group adjusted the timing of one transition rather than choosing one whole version unchanged. then they tested the new cut on someone who had not seen the earlier drafts. familiar viewers knew what to look for and could no longer provide the same kind of test. that difference helped the group understand why fresh feedback sometimes reveals problems that repeated rehearsal hides.

the engineering club wanted to show that a new handle was easier to grip. they first asked only the students who had helped design it. those students knew the intended grip and adjusted their hands without noticing. a visitor held the handle differently and found a sharp edge. the team invited several unfamiliar users to try it and watched where they hesitated. the next design rounded the edge and made the grip position more obvious. the designers had not been dishonest in the first test, but their familiarity had hidden a limitation. recording who took part in a test turned out to matter as much as recording the result.

one evening, a student noticed a wet patch beside a window in the study room. the first explanation was a leak from the frame. another student pointed to an empty bottle on the table. they did not have enough evidence to choose between the two. the room assistant documented the location and checked again after the table was dry. the next observation was more informative than an immediate label would have been. in her notebook, maya separated the visible wet patch from the possible causes. the distinction seemed small, but it prevented a tentative explanation from becoming a fact merely because someone had written it down.

a poster draft used a large arrow to connect two results. a classmate asked whether the arrow meant time order, cause, or simply a comparison. the team had used it because it looked clean, not because they had agreed on its meaning. they replaced the arrow with a short label and adjusted the layout. the figure took slightly more space, but the relationship was now explicit. later, they checked every symbol in the poster with the same question. a diagram could be visually simple and still be ambiguous. clarity required more than fewer words; it required the right information at the points where interpretation could diverge.

the final week of the project began with a list of unfinished tasks. some were essential for the report, while others were interesting extensions. the group marked each task according to whether the main conclusion depended on it. they finished the missing comparison before polishing the opening animation. when time ran short, they moved the extension to a section on future work. that did not make the extension unimportant. it made the boundary of the completed work honest. at the presentation, a visitor asked about the extension, and the group could explain exactly what remained to be tested rather than improvising an answer.

a student tested a scheduling tool with a calendar that contained only ordinary class meetings. every suggestion looked sensible. then she added a travel day, a cancelled meeting, and a task with no fixed time. the tool handled some cases well and misunderstood others. she saved the examples before changing the instructions. later, she used a different set of cases for the final check. otherwise, she would have measured how well she had repaired those particular examples rather than how well the new instructions handled unfamiliar ones. the distinction helped her report a modest improvement without claiming that the scheduling problem had been solved.

the studio windows let in strong afternoon light, making the projector difficult to read. the students first increased the brightness, then noticed that thin lines still disappeared. they changed the slide design by thickening important lines and increasing the contrast between neighboring shapes. a photograph taken from the back row showed which labels remained too small. the final rehearsal used the actual room rather than a laptop screen. the content had not changed, but the audience could now see it. a good explanation needed both a sound idea and a presentation that survived the conditions under which people would encounter it.

when the group cleaned the workshop, they found a box marked miscellaneous. it contained screws, cable ties, small wheels, and several pieces nobody recognized. sorting the known items was easy. for the others, the students created a separate tray and added photographs to the shared inventory. they did not invent names just to make every field complete. the tray remained useful because it preserved the items and the uncertainty together. a week later, another team recognized two of the pieces. the inventory could be updated without undoing an earlier guess. leaving something unknown had made later correction simpler, not harder.

a student compared two study plans for the next week. one allocated every available hour, leaving no room for delays. the other included short breaks and a spare block before the deadline. the first plan promised more completed work, but only under ideal conditions. after a discussion with a classmate, the student chose the second plan and identified which task could be postponed if necessary. the week still brought interruptions. having a planned adjustment made them easier to handle. she judged the plan by what it helped her finish, not by how impressive the original schedule looked when every hour was filled.

the sound of the workshop changed as students packed away their tools. a steady background noise faded, leaving brief clicks from latches and footsteps in the corridor. jo recorded a few seconds for the group's audio project, then asked everyone to stand still for a second recording. comparing the two clips revealed which sounds came from movement and which remained in the room. the recordings were not interchangeable. one documented the atmosphere of the cleanup; the other helped isolate the background. the group kept both and labeled their purpose, avoiding the temptation to treat a quieter recording as automatically better for every use.

a group reviewing its final results found one unusually successful run. it was tempting to show only that example because it made the project look polished. instead, they placed it beside a typical run and a failure. the three examples revealed different aspects of the system. the successful one showed what was possible, the typical one showed what a user might expect, and the failure showed where the design still needed work. their explanation became more interesting, not less. visitors asked specific questions because they could see the boundary between the intended behavior and the behavior the group had actually observed.

maya returned to the library table after the presentation. the notebook's first page still contained the three boxes she had drawn at the beginning. the box for ideas was crowded, and the box for evidence contained references to measurements, photographs, and conversations. the last box, for things nobody understood yet, was not empty. she did not regard that as a failure. some questions had become more precise, and others had led to useful experiments. before closing the notebook, she wrote a short message for the next group: begin with a clear question, keep the evidence, and make room for what you have not learned yet.
